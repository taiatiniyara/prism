How KPI formulas work — a plain-language guide

For the person responsible for KPI correctness: how to write a formula, how to point it at different energy providers and sources, and how to choose which level of the hierarchy the answer is computed for.

Part 1

Every KPI is three declarations, not one

When you define a KPI you are not just writing a formula. You are handing the calculator three separate pieces of information, and each answers a different question:

1

The recipe

How do the numbers combine? One line of arithmetic written with named ingredients — only + − × ÷ and brackets. There is no “SUM” function; summing happens automatically (Part 3).

2

The ingredient list

Where does each name in the recipe get its number? Each ingredient names one data item plus optional filters — energy provider, energy source, energy type. This is where multi-source formulas are expressed.

3

The output level

Which rung of the ladder does the answer belong to? Equipment, Power Station, Grid, Utility, Country… This tells the calculator how far to add things up before it applies the recipe.

Here is a complete definition for Renewable Share of Generation, the example used throughout this guide:

generation_renewable ÷ (generation_renewable + generation_conventional) × 100
generation_renewable reads the data item Electricity Generated Filters — Provider: any · Source: any · Type: Renewable
generation_conventional reads the data item Electricity Generated Filters — Provider: any · Source: any · Type: Conventional

Notice that both ingredients read the same data item. What makes them different numbers is the filter — and what keeps them apart in the recipe is that each filter combination has its own distinct name. That is the single most important rule in this guide: the calculator matches numbers to ingredients purely by name. Two ingredients with the same name collide, and the formula silently computes nonsense.

⚡ Generation data is entered here, at level 1: each row is tagged with its generator (which implies its power station and grid), its energy source, and its provider.

Two things to hold onto:

You define the formula once. The same recipe and ingredient list serve every level. Choosing the output level does not change the formula — it changes how many rows are added up before the recipe runs.

Levels 6 and 7 need nothing from you. The locked design decision is that the reporting layer produces Subregion and Region answers by adding up the same ingredients across countries and re-applying your stored recipe. The same rule applies to time: monthly ingredients are added into the financial year first, and the recipe is applied to the yearly sums — the system never averages twelve monthly percentages.

Part 3

The golden rule: add up the ingredients, then apply the recipe

This is the rule that makes one formula correct at every level, and it is worth seeing with real numbers. Suppose two stations report fuel efficiency (kWh generated per litre of fuel):

StationGenerated (kWh)Fuel used (L)Efficiency
Station A (small)10,0003,0003.33 kWh/L
Station B (large)90,00020,0004.50 kWh/L

What is the efficiency of the two stations combined?

✗ Wrong — average the answers
(3.33 + 4.50) ÷ 2 = 3.92 kWh/L

This treats the small station as if it mattered as much as one nine times its size. The error grows with every level you climb.

✓ Right — add ingredients, then divide
(10,000 + 90,000) ÷ (3,000 + 20,000) = 4.35 kWh/L

Each station influences the answer in proportion to its actual size. This is what the calculator does, at every level, always.

So when you see a KPI defined as a ratio or a percentage, remember: the division is applied after the summing, once, at the output level you chose. That is also why the recipe never contains a “SUM” — the ladder does the summing for you.

Part 4

Telling the formula about providers, sources and types

Every equipment-level row carries three labels alongside its number:

LabelWhat it saysExamples
Energy providerWho owns the generationUtility-owned IPP
Energy sourceWhat fuel or resourceDiesel · HFO · Hydro · Solar · Wind
Energy typeThe family the source belongs toConventional Renewable

Type is never entered — it is derived. Every source belongs to exactly one type (Diesel → Conventional; Hydro, Solar, Wind → Renewable), so a filter of Type = Renewable automatically covers every renewable source, including ones added in the future. Filter by type when you mean the family; filter by source only when the KPI is genuinely about one fuel.

One data item, many ingredients

To use the same data item several ways in one recipe, you create one named ingredient per filter combination — the same pantry item scooped through different sieves. An IPP share KPI, for example:

generation_ipp ÷ (generation_ipp + generation_utility) × 100
generation_ipp Electricity Generated Filters — Provider: IPP · Source: any · Type: any
generation_utility Electricity Generated Filters — Provider: Utility-owned · Source: any · Type: any

⚠ “Blank” does not mean “everything”

In the engine today, leaving a filter blank means “match rows that carry no label at all” — not “match every row”. Equipment-level rows always carry labels, so an unfiltered ingredient pointed at equipment data finds nothing, and the KPI fails with “missing input”.

Until an explicit “Any” option exists (recommended to the dev team — Part 7), express total by adding the filtered parts in the recipe, exactly as the two examples above do: generation_renewable + generation_conventional covers everything, because every source is either one or the other.

Part 5

Worked example: from generator rows to a grid-level KPI

KPI: Renewable Share of Generation · Output level: Grid / Service Area (level 3) · June 2026. The Viti Levu grid has four generating units that reported this month:

The calculator works through four steps:

  1. Gather

    Collect every row for the data items the ingredients name, within the scope being calculated — here, the Viti Levu grid for June 2026. All four rows qualify.

  2. Filter into buckets — one per ingredient

    generation_renewable (Type = Renewable) takes the green rows: Wailoa 7,500 and Qeleloa 1,500.
    generation_conventional (Type = Conventional) takes the amber rows: Kinoya 6,200 and 4,800.

  3. Add up each bucket to the output level

    The KPI's output level (Grid, level 3) is above the data's level (Equipment, level 1), so each bucket is summed: generation_renewable = 9,000 · generation_conventional = 11,000.

  4. Apply the recipe — once

    9,000 ÷ (9,000 + 11,000) × 100 = 45%. The division happens after the summing, never before.

The same definition, climbing the ladder

Now suppose the utility's other grid, Vanua Levu, reported 1,000 MWh renewable and 3,000 MWh conventional. Nothing about the KPI definition changes — only how far the buckets are summed before the recipe runs:

Viti Levu grid

Note the utility answer is 41.7%, not 35% (the average of 45 and 25) — the big grid rightly dominates. If you ever spot a published multi-level KPI where the higher level equals the plain average of the lower ones, that is a red flag worth investigating.

Part 6

What you write down: the KPI spec sheet

You do not need to touch the database to own formula correctness. For each KPI, hand the team a spec sheet with these six fields — it maps one-to-one onto what the engine stores:

FieldRenewable Share of Generation (filled in)
Question it answersWhat share of electricity generated came from renewable sources?

Unit%

Hand-checked answerViti Levu, June 2026: expect 45% (9,000 ÷ 20,000)

Correctness checklist — run it on every formula

  • Every name in the recipe appears in the ingredient list — and no two ingredients share a name.
  • Each ingredient's three filters are written out explicitly, even when they are "any".
  • "Total across everything" is expressed as a sum of filtered parts (e.g. renewable + conventional), never as a blank filter.
  • The output level is chosen deliberately — it is the level the question is asked at, not the level the data is entered at.
  • The unit matches the recipe's direction (kWh per litre is not litres per kWh — this exact inversion is live in one current KPI).
  • The sheet includes one hand-computed answer from real data, to compare against the engine's first output.

Part 7

Current-state watch-outs

Found while verifying the engine's behaviour against the live definitions — the first two affect published numbers today.

IssueWhat it means for you
Two live KPIs break the naming rule IPP Generation and Renewable Energy to Grid currently use the same ingredient name for numerator and denominator with no filters — so each computes a number divided by itself: always 100%. They need re-authoring using the pattern in Part 4.
One stored answer per KPI per period Today the results table keeps a single value per KPI per reporting period — it has no column saying which station or grid the value belongs to. A KPI computed for two grids overwrites itself. The planned reporting layer fixes this (results keyed by level and place); until then, treat sub-utility KPI values with caution.
Power Station is not yet a pickable level The system knows which station each generator belongs to, but level 2 is not currently offered as a KPI output level. Spec sheets can still be written against it — flag them so the dev team wires in the level.
No "Any" filter option yet Worth requesting: an explicit "Any" choice per filter, so totals stop depending on the sum-of-parts workaround (and on every source being correctly assigned to a type).
